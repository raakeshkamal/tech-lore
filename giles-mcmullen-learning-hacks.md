# Giles McMullen's Learning & Study Playbook

Extracted from 75 videos of his channel (@gilesmcmullen / Python Programmer). Pure learning/studying techniques and habits only — tutorials, sponsors and career talk stripped out. Sponsor reads (Brilliant, boot.dev, DataCamp, Shortform) are excluded except where he made a method point; his repeated "interactive learning is 6× more effective than video" claim is a sponsor marketing line — treat it as an ad, the underlying point (active > passive) is real and covered below.

Citations are video dates, e.g. `(20231031)`. Full title + link for every date is in §17 Sources.

---

## 1. The core method — what actually works

- **Retrieval practice (test yourself) — the single most effective technique he covers.** You don't remember by trying to remember. Reading, rereading, highlighting and copying don't commit things to memory; retrieving does. After reading a page, close the book, set a 5-minute timer, and write everything you can recall — keep going the full 5 minutes, put it in your own words, connect it to what you already know, then check the text for what you missed. Any format counts: free recall, quizzes, flashcards, questions. One session gives memory improvements lasting ~9 months; multiple sessions, effects measurable for years. Testing improves *understanding*, not just recall. (20231031, 20240620, 20240730, 20250425)
- **Flashcards done right:** produce and fully explain the answer *before* turning the card — no half-recall cheating. (20250425)
- **Pre-testing:** attempting questions on a subject you know nothing about prepares your brain to absorb the material. (20231031)
- **Spaced practice (never cram).** Same study hours spread over days/weeks beats one long session by a mile: students who spread 12 problems over 3 days scored ~2× higher a month later on new problems. Match spacing to your retention goal — to remember something for a week, space sessions 12–24h apart; for 5 years, 6–12 months apart. Bonus: start each session by retrieving the previous session's material. (20231031, 20231231, 20250425)
- **Interleaving (mix topics).** Don't block-practice one topic then the next — mix problem types within a session. Blocked practice feels better (89% vs 60% accuracy while practising) but on a test a week later it collapses (20% vs 63%). Mixing forces you to first work out *what kind* of problem you're facing — that discrimination is the skill. Students hate it because it feels worse; it's "ridiculously effective". (20231031, 20231231, 20250425)
- **Elaborative interrogation — ask how and why constantly.** While reading anything, keep asking: *How does this work? Why is it true? How does it relate to what I already know? What would happen without it?* Write down your answers. This fits new knowledge into your existing knowledge network, which is where understanding (and memory) lives. (20240620, 20231031, 20250916)
- **Elaboration / self-explanation:** explain concepts out loud in your own words, and talk yourself through *why each step* of a problem works as you solve it. (20250916, 20250425)
- **Desirable difficulty.** Effective learning feels hard; if your studying feels easy and smooth, you're not learning. The struggle to retrieve, to discriminate between problems, to explain without notes — that effort is the mechanism. Don't sand it away. (20250904, 20250425, 20250916, 20260129)
- **Concrete examples.** Abstract concepts don't stick; ground every one in an example you can visualise. If the material gives no examples, make your own. (20250425)
- **Dual coding:** learn with words *and* pictures — labelled diagrams, timelines. Find them or draw them. (20250425)
- **Generation:** attempt answers before you're taught (prediction forces engagement). (20221026)
- **Rest immediately after a study session.** The brain digests new input during downtime; hit it with new input (especially your phone) right after learning and you disrupt consolidation. (20241026)

## 2. What doesn't work (the illusions)

- **Highlighting and rereading are near-useless.** The 2013 review of 10 popular techniques found most ineffective — and the *less* effective a technique was, the more students liked it. Rereading: no benefit after the second read. Highlighting: can actually hinder deeper learning. His confession: he highlighted nearly every line of his university textbooks — "just coloring the pages". (20250916, 20231031, 20221026)
- **The familiarity trap.** These passive methods create fluency with the *text*, and your brain mistakes "I recognise this" for "I understand this". Recognition ≠ knowledge — you can recite E=mc² without understanding anything underneath it. When learning, ask: is this just notebook knowledge, or have I made it my own? (20250916, 20250531, 20231231)
- **The "yeah I get it" illusion.** Racing through a worked example feels like understanding; the next day you can't reproduce it. Working memory is deceiving you (see §7). (20231231)
- **You cannot will yourself to remember.** Staring at a page repeating "I must remember this" does nothing. Use the methods in §1 instead. (20240620, 20250425)
- **Watching ≠ learning.** Consuming videos/lectures passively absorbs nothing. The test: *was what I just did passive?* If yes, you weren't learning. Active engagement requires answering questions, explaining, creating. (20251015, 20250731)

## 3. Bloom's taxonomy — the 6-step roadmap to expertise

He frames this as the map for learning *anything*, "no talent required" — know where you are and push upward; most people spend their lives in the bottom half.

1. **Remember** — memorise the raw basics (syntax, formulas, vocabulary). Flashcards, self-testing. (20240327)
2. **Understand** — ask why, why, why about every element until you know *why* each part is there. (20240327)
3. **Apply** — do the thing: write the code, solve the equation. "People think they can learn by reading or watching — your brain is tricking you. Don't be tricked. Do the thing." (20240327)
4. **Analyse** — take it apart: what does each component do, what happens if you change/remove/substitute it? (20240327)
5. **Evaluate** — judge quality: critique your own work and others', read multiple sources and form your own view. (20240327)
6. **Create** — make something original that combines it all. Expertise lives in steps 4–6. (20240327, 20250731)

Notes: the levels aren't strictly sequential in practice — they interleave. And writing an essay (§4) forces every level, which is why he calls it the perfect learning exercise. (20250731, 20250904)

## 4. Write to learn (his highest-leverage advice)

- **Write essays on what you're learning.** Not school essays — exploratory writing for yourself. Start with a thought, follow it, make connections. The essay forces remember→understand→apply→analyse→evaluate→create, embeds retrieval, spacing, interleaving and elaboration in one activity, and builds the knowledge into your own scaffold. "If you want to learn something quickly and well, write an essay on it." Start small: short, specific topic, an interesting angle — don't attempt 5,000 words. (20240730, 20250904)
- **Write analytically, then interrogate yourself.** Don't write descriptively ("X happened in 1066") — write claims ("X transformed England forever"), then challenge your own claims: how, why, how significant, what's the counterargument, does it hold? (20240730)
- **Writing-to-learn (Zinsser):** writing exposes holes in your knowledge — "am I saying what I want to say?" is very often no, and forcing the answer teaches you. Two modes: explanatory (explain what you know) and exploratory (write freely about how it relates to you and what you don't yet get). Works for maths and science, not just humanities. Expect it to be painful — "I don't like to write, but I take great pleasure in having written." (20240226)
- **Keep a learning journal:** frequently summarise what you've learned *without looking at the source*, and write a little essay on how it fits with what you already knew. (20240529)
- **Close-the-book notes (retrieval notetaking):** read a page, close the book, write everything you can remember from it. Beats copying notes word-for-word every time. (20240730)
- **Teach it or blog it.** Explaining something to someone forces you to actually understand it, and their questions show you angles you missed. A learning blog compounds the same effect in public. (20190110, 20200120)

## 5. Note-taking systems

- **Take formal notes by hand.** A 2014 study: longhand notetakers outperform laptop notetakers on tests — shorter, higher-quality notes, better encoding. (20250624)
- **His hand-notes process for a book chapter:** (1) skim first paragraph, headings, last paragraph; (2) write down what you think the chapter is about and how it might fit what you know; (3) read in sections, pulling out main points with secondary points nested under them; (4) write as little as possible — key ideas and arguments only; (5) constantly question the argument, look for exceptions, connect to what you know. Treat the chapter like a whodunit you're solving. (20250624)
- **Marginalia:** write in the margins as a dialogue with the text — bracket sections, underline phrases that link to other ideas, note confusion and broken logic, summarise hard concepts in your own words. Let your reading purpose shape the annotations. Notes become memory-jogs when you revisit. (20250624)
- **Question-first notes:** jot questions down, then note the answers as you read; always record source and page number. (20240529)
- **Linked notes (Obsidian/Zettelkasten):** one idea per note; link each new note to related ones; tag for categories. The links between ideas spark new connections — a network effect for your knowledge, and nothing gets lost in dead documents. Start with just notes + links + tags; let your own workflow emerge before adding plugins/fancy methods. (20230929, 20240814)
- **Don't trap notes in "note prisons".** A Word doc's ideas die when the project ends; a linked vault carries them into everything you do after. Niklas Luhmann (70 books, ~400 papers) credited the Zettelkasten for his output. (20240814)
- **Summarising in your own words** is itself one of the highest-value memory techniques — every note should be a compression, not a transcription. (20230929, 20250624)
- **Don't over-engineer the tool.** Hours researching the perfect note app is procrastination wearing a productive costume. Pick one (he uses Obsidian), set up basics, start using it. (20240814)

## 6. Reading (his biggest theme in 2024–2026)

**Building the reading habit**
- **15 minutes a day ≈ 15 books a year.** An average book (~100k words) at average reading speed works out to ~15 min/day. Fix a slot for it — don't fit reading around other tasks. He reads every evening in the same spot with a cup of tea. Start with 15, stretch to 20–30. (20260131)
- **Start with easy books you love.** Don't open with the classics — that's skiing the black slope first. Plot-driven page-turners rebuild reading stamina; the hard stuff comes once you love reading again. (20260131)
- **Mix genres.** Every ~10th book, read something you'd never normally pick — especially fiction. He regrets abandoning humanities at 16 and only fixing it decades later. (20260131)
- **Carry a book everywhere; make reading more accessible than your phone.** Switch off notifications; spare five minutes go to the book, not the feed. (20260131, 20250317)
- **Join a library. Underused and underrated.** Get learning books from the library first; only buy what works for you. (20260131, 20240630)
- **Quit freely.** If you hate a book, stop and move on — pressure kills the habit. Read what interests you unapologetically. (20250317)
- **Realistic goals:** a novel a month or 10 min/day, not YouTuber flexes of a book a week. Consistency beats intensity. (20250317)
- **Why it matters (motivation):** reading is not innate like speech — it's a technology your brain physically rewires itself for. The 1970 British Cohort Study (~17,000 people tracked ages 5→42): reading for pleasure increased cognitive ability, with childhood impact ~4× greater than having degree-educated parents; a one-level reading lead at age 7 predicted ~£5k higher earnings at 42. The less you read, the harder reading gets (stamina decay) — the habit reverses the spiral. (20260131, 20250317)

**Reading actively (understanding, not word-scanning)**
- **Read for insight, not information.** Fact-grabbing ("Helsinki is Finland's capital") isn't understanding. Aim for reading that changes how you think. Reciting Newton's laws word-for-word isn't understanding them. (20260531)
- **Interrogate the text (semantic encoding).** While reading, keep asking: how does this relate to what I know? What are the applications? Where are the weak points? Reading is an active dialogue with the author — content consumption ≠ understanding. Applies equally to lectures, podcasts, videos. (20250624)
- **Understand → then you'll remember** — reverse the usual order (read→try to remember→maybe understand) into read→interrogate→understand→remember. A wandering mind while reading is the symptom of not engaging. (20250624)
- **The Socratic reading method (his 6-step routine for hard books):** before opening the book, write what you already believe about the subject (exposes your biases); read asking clarifying questions; probe assumptions and generate counterarguments; test interpretations against the evidence in the text; discuss the ideas with someone; reflect afterwards on what you learned and what new questions arose. (20240828)
- **Reverse-engineer the writer.** Try to get inside the author's head — text is thoughts, not words. Virginia Woolf's test: try *writing* the kind of passage you're reading; feeling how hard it is to turn thought into words makes you far better at turning words back into thought. Then judge the book: where does it succeed, where does it fail. (20260531)
- **Digest a few master thinkers instead of snacking on many.** Seneca: "linger among a limited number of master thinkers, and digest their works" — reading everywhere means knowing nowhere. Re-read the good ones; chew over what you read (Locke: "it is thinking makes what we read ours"). (20260531, 20251015)
- **Critical reading pass:** what's the argument, what assumptions does it rest on, does the conclusion follow, is the source reliable, what's the agenda? (20250627, 20240828)
- Not every read needs this treatment — sometimes curling up with a story is fine, and fine on purpose. (20260531)

**Speed reading (the debunk — worth knowing so you don't buy a course)**
- Word-at-a-glance page reading is biologically impossible (only the fovea resolves detail); silencing your inner voice *decreases* comprehension; blocking regressions (looking back) measurably hurts comprehension. Reading speed is limited by cognitive processing, not eye movement — so eye-training can't 10× it. (20231129)
- **The only real way to read faster: read more.** Familiar words get shorter fixations or get skipped entirely — vocabulary and language facility are what raise speed. (20231129)
- **Legit systematic skimming (2–3× speed, at a comprehension cost, for the right occasions):** read the table of contents first, headings, first and last paragraph of each chapter, first/last sentence of paragraphs in your target section. (20231129)

## 7. Memory mechanics

- **Working memory is tiny.** ~4–7 items for 15–30 seconds — it's RAM, not disk. Learning = moving information from working memory into long-term memory. Relying on working memory while reading/studying is why stuff evaporates. (20250624, 20231231)
- **"Race car drivers" vs "hikers":** people differ in working-memory capacity, but long-term memory is effectively limitless — deliberate-encoding strategies let weak-working-memory learners match or beat strong ones. (20231231)
- **Chunking:** group items into meaningful units to expand what working memory can hold. (20250624)
- **Memory palace (method of loci):** place items to remember in rooms of a familiar place — effective, especially for lists. (20250624)
- **Encode by meaning:** memory fails when encoding is shallow. Focus on meaning and context, use analogies for abstract ideas, integrate new ideas into your knowledge network — that's what makes recall near-total at the higher levels. (20250624)

## 8. Sleep, exercise and the body (the substrate of learning)

- **Sleep is the strongest performance lever.** MIT's Fitbit study of 100 chemistry students: zero correlation between exercise and grades, but a straight-line relationship between sleep and scores. ~6.5h/night → grades down ~50% vs one hour more. (20241218)
- **Consistency beats duration.** Bedtime *regularity* (weekends included) predicts outcomes better than total hours; students whose sleep varied by 30 min/night were down ~45%. Later bedtime is worse even at the same total — there's a cutoff around 2am. And it's the sleep you get *while learning*, not the night before the exam, that counts. (20241218, 20250121)
- **Sleep does the consolidation:** short→long-term memory transfer, synapse reset, waste clearance. All-nighters are actively self-sabotage; even a nap helps. (20241026, 20250121, 20231231)
- **Squats: his "single best exercise for the brain"** (per Prof Damian Bailey) — up/down movement against gravity drives blood flow to the hippocampus and boosts BDNF, which supports new neuron growth. (20241026)
- **Micro-breaks:** ~2 minutes of genuine mind-wandering (not phone-scrolling) every ~20 minutes. Surgeons taking 5-min breaks every 30 made fewer errors in the same total time. (20241026)
- **Walk, ideally in the morning** — light resets the body clock (better sleep) and raises serotonin. (20210914)
- **Learn an instrument.** Full-brain workout with measurable transfer to memory, executive function and processing speed, plus lower stress/anxiety. (20250328)

## 9. Habits & consistency

- **Willpower is the wrong tool.** Goal-pursuit by constant willpower battle mostly fails; design habits instead. ~43% of daily behaviour is already habitual — habits form whether you design them or not, so design them. (20240117)
- **Environment > motivation (friction engineering).** Make the good habit easy and the bad one hard: fruit bowl replaces the biscuit tin; dedicated study spot with everything ready; study at the library. Evidence: people living 3.7 miles from a gym went 5×/month; at 5.1 miles, once a month — tiny friction differences, huge behaviour differences. (20240117)
- **~66 days of repetition** to make a behaviour automatic (on average) — keep the environment supportive until then. (20240117)
- **Reward at the moment of doing,** not in the distant future (hour of study → favourite show). The reward gradually becomes intrinsic. (20240117)
- **Small daily sessions, realistic timelines.** 10–30 minutes a day beats heroic bursts; accept that real proficiency takes 6–18 months and don't set yourself up to quit by over-ambition. (20190720, 20200831)
- **Code/practise every single day.** Never let a day pass without learning one concept or applying one. Consistency is the whole game. (20200120, 20220504)
- **Concrete, specific goals beat vague ones.** "Learn to code" fails; "learn Python to build this specific app / analyse this dataset" sustains motivation. His mental-maths project: "square 57 in under 10 seconds" — measurable. (20220120, 20240424)
- **Start now.** "Too old" is a perspective trap: in five years you'll wish you'd started today. Bloom's research: experts are made by deliberate practice, not born. (20200120, 20190629)
- **Self-efficacy: believing you can learn it measurably raises the odds you will** (kids given maths-ability-belief support alongside maths tutoring improved most). (20241026)
- **Growth mindset for maths specifically:** inability at maths is usually a bad teacher's legacy, not a fact about you. (20190110)

## 10. Focus & environment

- **Strip the study space.** He keeps an office with only a desk, paper, pen, laptop — no kitchen, no TV, no instruments — and gets ~10× the work done there. Remove distractions *before* every session; attention is requirement #1 for learning. (20240630, 20190110, 20250731)
- **Don't "study with" YouTubers.** Watching other people study isn't studying, and the algorithm is engineered to keep you on the platform. (20190110)
- **Phone: notifications off, out of reach.** (20250317)
- **Keyboard shortcuts are a compound-interest skill.** Fitts's law: a mouse targeting takes seconds, a shortcut ~300–500ms. Learn the top 10 for your OS and most-used apps; expect 20–30 repetitions before a shortcut beats the mouse — the initial slowdown is why most people quit. (20230116)
- **Touch typing** keeps your eyes on the screen and you in flow; ~1 hour/day for 2–3 weeks to learn, free (he used the BBC course). (20211102)
- **Search like a researcher:** `site:`, `filetype:`, `-word` to exclude, `"exact phrase"`, `intitle:`, `after:/before:`, `OR`, `related:`. Knowing how to find the thing that unsticks you is a core learning skill. (20221031, 20200120)

## 11. Procrastination

- **It's mood management, not laziness.** You procrastinate to escape the negative emotions attached to a task. Ask: am I avoiding the task, or the feelings about it? The relief is short-lived and followed by guilt — that's the loop. (20240630)
- **Self-compassion first, tactics second.** Studies show self-compassion reduces procrastination *and* raises motivation; shame and self-criticism do the opposite. His 3-step self-forgiveness: accept you procrastinated → allow the bad feeling → move on. (20240630)
- **Reconnect with your future self.** Procrastinators mythologise their future self as a superhuman who'll do it faster and better. It won't be you-with-superpowers; it'll be you. (20240630)
- **Distrust your intensity bias:** tasks feel far more unpleasant in anticipation than in execution. (20240630)
- **Define the task precisely, then shrink it.** Vague tasks invite delay. Break "write essay" into research → outline → intro → body → conclusion; each finished sub-step builds momentum. Start with the first small step. (20240630, 20240529)
- **The 5-minute rule: just start.** Begin now, even if only for 5 minutes. (20240630)
- **Add stakes and a real deadline.** Committing to perform your work to an audience of 200 (his stand-up year) makes procrastination unattractive — engineered eustress. (20250422)
- **Watch for over-research as procrastination.** The 10th video won't tell you what the first 9 didn't. Quantity of practice beats quality of preparation: in the photography-class experiment, the group graded on *quantity* of photos beat the "one perfect photo" group on both quantity AND quality — doing beats theorising. If you want to write, write — don't read about writing. (20251015, 20250630)
- **Trust the process, judge yourself at 1, 3 and 12 months** — not daily. Start today; you'll astound yourself. (20250630)

## 12. Deliberate practice & feedback

- **Practise at the thing itself.** To get better at solving problems, solve lots of problems — not adjacent activities. Time at the keyboard/with the pencil is where learning happens; books and tutorials only supply information. (20220509, 20240924)
- **Attack your weakest spots.** After a couple of weeks you know which topics you're bad at — put most of your time there. The temptation is to replay what you're already good at (the passages of the piece that sound good) because it feels better. He drilled his 7-times table when he found he couldn't square numbers in the 70s. (20240924, 20240424)
- **Fill-in-the-gaps practice (his D→A maths method):** work from problems with part of the solution already given and complete the rest yourself. When sheets run out, cover worked textbook solutions and reconstruct them. Escalate difficulty as soon as it gets easy. Result: predicted-D student → 99% mock, 2-year course compressed to 3 months. (20240924)
- **Problem sets ARE the learning, not the homework between learnings.** His physics mistake: hunting through hundreds of book pages for a matching worked example instead of wrestling with the problem. (20251015)
- **Feedback is half the loop.** Repetition needs motivation + feedback to compound. Find your "Mr Simpson" — someone who marks your work and tells you why it's wrong. Self-learners: use answer-checking, communities, or AI critique. (20240924, 20250630)
- **Test yourself under real conditions.** His plan for mental maths: retrieval practice + timed drills + an actual test (a friend firing random numbers at him, stopwatch running). (20240424)
- **Don't take the answer for the answer.** Getting it right isn't the point — know *why* it's right, and build the intuition behind the rule. (20240924)
- **Quantity with feedback beats quality without it** — see the photography experiment above; also Michelangelo: nobody would marvel if they saw the hours. "Even talent is hardly distinguishable over the long run from perseverance and lots of hard work." (20250630)
- **The creative cycle (Wallas, 1926):** preparation → incubation (step away; let the subconscious work) → illumination (capture the flash — have a notebook ready) → verification (then do the hard polishing work). Stepping away after focused effort is a technique, not slacking. (20241201, 20231231)

## 13. Learning to code (his core advice, distilled)

- **You're learning problem-solving, not a language.** The real skill is computational thinking: deciding whether/how a computer can solve a problem, breaking it into pieces a machine can execute. Read Jeannette Wing's 2006 "Computational Thinking" article. The language is just the vehicle — that's why your second language comes fast. (20190720, 20200820, 20200120, 20260129, 20220204)
- **Learn to a target — pick your "song".** Like an instrument, you learn scales in order to play a piece: decide what you want to *build* (app, site, robot, model). The project is both motivation and measure of progress. Choose several challenging-but-completable projects rather than one monster you'll never finish. (20220509, 20190103)
- **Stop doing endless tutorials.** Following along is watching someone else play the piano once. Cap syntax learning at ~one week, then solve problems — small useful scripts, games, challenges. Go off the tutorial path and find your own solutions to novel situations; that's where learning happens. (20260129, 20220509, 20220504)
- **Work code, don't read code.** For every snippet: get it running, then break it, then modify it, then ask why it was written that way and what else could do the job. (20220831)
- **Read other people's code** once you have footing — one of the fastest ways to level up. (20200831, 20231224)
- **Exercises for every concept.** After each new topic, do many exercises before moving on; hunt down challenge questions if the resource doesn't provide them. Do the *hard* exercises — the ones you'd rather skip. (20200831, 20220831)
- **Solve puzzles daily.** Project Euler, Codewars, HackerRank, Exercism, Python Challenge etc.; pick one site, stick with it, a problem a day or a few a week. Don't look up solutions — earn them. (20181227, 20220405)
- **Pick one resource and commit.** Resource-hopping is how beginners hide from the difficulty. Find material that suits you, then stay with it. (20200120)
- **Don't linger on the basics** — read them once, put them into practice, move to real problems. (20200120)
- **Data structures & algorithms through problems** (especially for jobs): learn them by solving problems that force them, so you learn *when* each fits. (20190720, 20200120)
- **Expect the wall.** Everyone hits a point where nothing makes sense and quitting feels inevitable; syntax was the easy part — the new way of thinking is the hard part, and it clicks suddenly if you keep going. Battling one concept for days/weeks is normal. (20220204)
- **Technical books: active protocol.** Computer beside you, notebook for deep-dive topics, work the code, do the hard exercises, realistic pace (a dense technical book takes months), never skip what you don't understand — research or ask until you do. Understanding beats memorising. Cap yourself at ~3 carefully chosen books; the perfect book doesn't exist. (20220831)
- **Learn to unstick yourself:** search well, find similar solved problems, build the skill of getting unstuck — but really try first, always. (20200120, 20200831)
- **Write a learning blog; learn git and share code publicly.** Explaining compounds understanding; a public track record compounds credibility. (20200120)
- **Interactive apps can work for basics** (text→run-and-play-with-the-code→quiz pattern), but a real computer is better because real projects are better. (20220916)

## 14. Using AI to learn (without cognitive debt)

**Use AI as a trainer, not a servant.** You wouldn't have your personal trainer run the track for you — the *doing* is the benefit. Whatever you do with AI, make it force you to think. (20250130)

His concrete AI study prompts:
- **Bespoke course generation:** state your level, goal, and timeline; ask for a structured course/path, then drill into any topic with follow-ups. (20230131, 20231224, 20230322)
- **Socratic tutor prompt:** "Act as a Socratic tutor and help me understand [concept]. Ask me questions to guide my understanding." Answer in your own words; it finds the gaps. (20250130)
- **Multi-level explanation drill:** have it explain a concept to a child, then a high-schooler, then academically — then *you* write explanations at each level and have it grade yours. (20250130, 20230322)
- **Generate quizzes and graded challenges** at the difficulty and Bloom level you name; have it build a question ladder up the taxonomy. Attempting its tests is retrieval practice. (20230131, 20231224, 20250130)
- **When stuck, ask for advice, not the answer** — have it break the problem into steps and question you, instead of writing the solution. (20230322)
- **Summarise yourself first, then compare.** Never let AI summarise for you — you lose the skill and won't see what it missed. Write your own summary, ask it for its own, compare the differences. (20250130)
- **Concept extraction for hard papers:** ask for the 20 key terms in 5 categories, or the propositions as "X is a type of Y / W is caused by X" in a table — a checklist of what you must understand. (20250130)
- **Ask without embarrassment** — re-explain endlessly, no classroom shame. A slightly-unreliable tutor is still a tutor: always sanity-check output (hallucinations). (20250130)
- **Early on, do NOT let it write your code.** Get feedback, questions and explanations from it; write the code yourself. "Before using autopilot, you should know how to fly the plane." (20260129)

**The warning (MIT Media Lab studies he covers):** LLM users writing essays showed ~half the brain connectivity of unassisted writers; 83% couldn't quote their own essay; the deficit persisted when they later wrote unassisted — "cognitive debt". Offloading a skill erodes the offloaded ability, and AI now lets you offload thinking itself; heavy AI use correlates with reduced critical thinking, worst in the youngest users. (20251207, 20260531)

**His protection protocol:** attempt first, *then* use AI — summarise/test yourself/write the short essay before asking the machine; critically evaluate everything it produces. (20251207)

## 15. Mindset & the thinking habits that support learning

- **Fix mental health before technique.** Learning techniques don't work while you're anxious or sliding; notice it early and get support. (He credits bottling it up for dropping out of university at 18.) (20240924)
- **Curiosity is the engine.** Follow it and keep asking why/how/what-if — the habit of questioning everything you consume is what separates active learners from passive ones. (20241111, 20250831, 20250916)
- **Evaluate ideas like airport security:** let them in only after inspection — what's the claim, does the conclusion follow, what's the evidence, is the source reliable, is persuasive language substituting for evidence? Don't slide into cynicism; be open, then rigorous. (20241111)
- **Question your own beliefs the same way:** am I right? why do I believe this? what evidence? what assumptions? Treat outrage as a manipulation alarm. (20250627)
- **The trivium in one line:** grammar (say precisely what things are) → logic (does the conclusion follow?) → rhetoric (express it) — train the thinking tools and every subject gets easier. His suggested entry point: Dorothy Sayers' essay "The Lost Tools of Learning". (20260519)
- **Make maxims, don't memorise them (Seneca).** Collecting quotes and highlights is storing other people's property; knowledge is making ideas your own and creating with them. "Remembering is merely safeguarding something entrusted to the memory; knowing means making everything your own." (20250531)
- **Recreate the Oxford tutorial for yourself:** assign a weekly topic, research it, write an essay with your OWN argument, then cross-examine yourself as the tutor would — attack your argument, find its weaknesses, respond. If friends study the same thing, hold peer-review sessions and challenge each other. (20250831)
- **Find your scenius.** Intelligence is generated by communities, not just individuals — a study group, a community, a mentor multiplies you. (20250831)
- **Bertrand Russell's discipline (condensed to the learning-relevant three):** don't feel absolutely certain of anything; meet opposition with argument, not authority; find more pleasure in intelligent dissent than in passive agreement. (20241111)

## 16. The bookshelf (learning-science canon he actually recommends)

**How learning works**
- *Make It Stick* — Brown, Roediger & McDaniel. The science-backed canon: retrieval, spacing, interleaving, elaboration. His default recommendation if you read one. (20230929, 20231031, 20240529)
- *Understanding How We Learn* — Weinstein & Sumeracki (the Learning Scientists). ~100 pages, evidence-based and *prescriptive* — shows you HOW to apply each technique. His current top pick. Their website (learningscientists.org) is free. (20250430, 20231031)
- *Ultralearning* — Scott Young. 9-step aggressive self-study method; same method Giles stumbled on for his maths rescue. (20240924)
- *Outsmart Your Brain* — Daniel Willingham (the Oxford-recommended study-skills book he found): time management, procrastination, note-taking, exams, memory, ~400 pages. (20240529)

**Writing, reading, thinking**
- *On Writing Well* + *Writing to Learn* — William Zinsser. (20240226)
- *The Socratic Method* — Ward Farnsworth. (20240828)
- *How to Read a Book* — Adler & Van Doren; *The Trivium* — Sister Miriam Joseph; "The Lost Tools of Learning" — Sayers essay. (20260519)
- *Letters from a Stoic* — Seneca (esp. "On the Futility of Learning Maxims"). (20250531, 20260531)
- *Reader, Come Home / The Story and Science of the Reading Brain* — Maryanne Wolf. (20260131)
- Essay collections to sharpen thinking: Montaigne's *Complete Essays*, Woolf *A Room of One's Own*, Zadie Smith *Feel Free*, Pullman *Daemon Voices*, Runciman *A History of Ideas*, Saunders *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*, Baggini *How to Think Like a Philosopher*. (20260113, 20241201)

**Habits, sleep, maths, code**
- *Good Habits, Bad Habits* — Wendy Wood (his favourite habit book); *Atomic Habits* — Clear (more of an instruction manual); *Tiny Habits* — Fogg (most practical). (20240117)
- *Why We Sleep* — Matthew Walker. (20250121)
- *Think Like a Math Genius* — Arthur Benjamin (mental-maths tricks; see his TED talk). (20240424, 20240430)
- *The Art of Statistics* — Spiegelhalter; *Code* — Petzold (mental model of computers); *An Introduction to Statistical Learning* — free PDF. (20200824, 20210701, 20200911)
- *How to Take Smart Notes* — Sönke Ahrens (Zettelkasten; he rates the principles/steps sections over the technique section). (20240814)
- Dr Sirois' procrastination book (self-compassion-first approach). (20240630)
